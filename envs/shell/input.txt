<p>The <b>Cray-1</b> was a <a href="/wiki/Supercomputer" title="Supercomputer">supercomputer</a> designed, manufactured and marketed by <a href="/wiki/Cray_Research" class="mw-redirect" title="Cray Research">Cray Research</a>. Announced in 1975, the first Cray-1 system was installed at <a href="/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory" title="Los Alamos National Laboratory">Los Alamos National Laboratory</a> in 1976. Eventually, eighty Cray-1s were sold, making it one of the most successful supercomputers in history. It is perhaps best known for its unique shape, a relatively small C-shaped cabinet with a ring of benches around the outside covering the power supplies and the cooling system.
</p><p>The Cray-1 was the first supercomputer to successfully implement the <a href="/wiki/Vector_processor" title="Vector processor">vector processor</a> design. These systems improve the performance of math operations by arranging memory and <a href="/wiki/Processor_register" title="Processor register">registers</a> to quickly perform a single operation on a large set of data. Previous systems like the <a href="/wiki/CDC_STAR-100" title="CDC STAR-100">CDC STAR-100</a> and <a href="/wiki/TI_Advanced_Scientific_Computer" title="TI Advanced Scientific Computer">ASC</a> had implemented these concepts but did so in a way that seriously limited their performance. The Cray-1 addressed these problems and produced a machine that ran several times faster than any similar design.
</p><p>The Cray-1's architect was <a href="/wiki/Seymour_Cray" title="Seymour Cray">Seymour Cray</a>; the chief engineer was Cray Research co-founder Lester Davis.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>2<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> They would go on to design several new machines using the same basic concepts, and retained the performance crown into the 1990s.
</p>

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="History">History</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray-1&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>From 1968 to 1972, Seymour Cray of <a href="/wiki/Control_Data_Corporation" title="Control Data Corporation">Control Data Corporation</a> (CDC) worked on the <a href="/wiki/CDC_8600" title="CDC 8600">CDC 8600</a>, the successor to his earlier <a href="/wiki/CDC_6600" title="CDC 6600">CDC 6600</a> and <a href="/wiki/CDC_7600" title="CDC 7600">CDC 7600</a> designs. The 8600 was essentially made up of four 7600s in a box with an additional special mode that allowed them to operate <a href="/wiki/Lockstep_(computing)" title="Lockstep (computing)">lock-step</a> in a <a href="/wiki/SIMD" class="mw-redirect" title="SIMD">SIMD</a> fashion.
</p><p>Jim Thornton, formerly Cray's engineering partner on earlier designs, had started a more radical project known as the <a href="/wiki/CDC_STAR-100" title="CDC STAR-100">CDC STAR-100</a>. Unlike the 8600's brute-force approach to performance, the STAR took an entirely different route. The main processor of the STAR had lower performance than the 7600, but added hardware and instructions to speed up particularly common supercomputer tasks.
</p><p>By 1972, the 8600 had reached a dead end; the machine was so incredibly complex that it was impossible to get one working properly. Even a single faulty component would render the machine non-operational. Cray went to <a href="/wiki/William_Norris_(CEO)" title="William Norris (CEO)">William Norris</a>, Control Data's CEO, saying that a redesign from scratch was needed. At the time, the company was in serious financial trouble, and with the STAR in the pipeline as well, Norris could not invest the money.
</p><p>As a result, Cray left CDC and started <a href="/wiki/Cray_Research" class="mw-redirect" title="Cray Research">Cray Research</a> very close to the CDC lab. In the back yard of the land he purchased in <a href="/wiki/Chippewa_Falls,_Wisconsin" title="Chippewa Falls, Wisconsin">Chippewa Falls, Wisconsin</a>, Cray and a group of former CDC employees started looking for ideas. At first, the concept of building another supercomputer seemed impossible, but after Cray Research's <a href="/wiki/Chief_technology_officer" title="Chief technology officer">Chief Technology Officer</a> travelled to <a href="/wiki/Wall_Street" title="Wall Street">Wall Street</a> and found a lineup of investors willing to back Cray, all that was needed was a design.
</p><p>For four years Cray Research designed its first computer.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>3<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> In 1975 the 80&#160;MHz Cray-1 was announced. The excitement was so high that a bidding war for the first machine broke out between <a href="/wiki/Lawrence_Livermore_National_Laboratory" title="Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory">Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory</a> and <a href="/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory" title="Los Alamos National Laboratory">Los Alamos National Laboratory</a>, the latter eventually winning and receiving serial number 001 in 1976 for a six-month trial. The <a href="/wiki/National_Center_for_Atmospheric_Research" title="National Center for Atmospheric Research">National Center for Atmospheric Research</a> (NCAR) was the first official customer of Cray Research in 1977, paying US$8.86 million ($7.9 million plus $1 million for the disks) for serial number 3. The NCAR machine was decommissioned in 1989.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>4<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> The company expected to sell perhaps a dozen of the machines, and set the selling price accordingly, but ultimately over 80 Cray-1s of all types were sold, priced from $5M to $8M. The machine made Seymour Cray a celebrity and his company a success, lasting until the supercomputer crash in the early 1990s.
</p><p>Based on a recommendation by <a href="/wiki/William_J._Perry" title="William J. Perry">William Perry</a>'s study, the <a href="/wiki/National_Security_Agency" title="National Security Agency">NSA</a> purchased a Cray-1 for theoretical research in <a href="/wiki/Cryptanalysis" title="Cryptanalysis">cryptanalysis</a>. According to Budiansky, "Though standard histories of Cray Research would persist for decades in stating that the company's first customer was Los Alamos National Laboratory, in fact it was NSA..."<sup id="cite&#95;ref-Stephen&#95;5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Stephen-5"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>5<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>The 160&#160;<a href="/wiki/FLOPS" class="mw-redirect" title="FLOPS">MFLOPS</a> Cray-1 was succeeded in 1982 by the 800&#160;MFLOPS <a href="/wiki/Cray_X-MP" title="Cray X-MP">Cray X-MP</a>, the first Cray multi-processing computer. In 1985, the very advanced <a href="/wiki/Cray-2" title="Cray-2">Cray-2</a>, capable of 1.9&#160;GFLOPS peak performance, succeeded the first two models but met a somewhat limited commercial success because of certain problems at producing sustained performance in real-world applications. A more conservatively designed evolutionary successor of the Cray-1 and X-MP models was therefore made by the name <a href="/wiki/Cray_Y-MP" title="Cray Y-MP">Cray Y-MP</a> and launched in 1988.
</p><p>By comparison, the processor in a typical 2013 smart device, such as a <a href="/wiki/Google" title="Google">Google</a> <a href="/wiki/Nexus_10" title="Nexus 10">Nexus 10</a> or <a href="/wiki/HTC_One_(M7)" title="HTC One (M7)">HTC One</a>, performs at roughly 1 GFLOPS,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>6<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> while the <a href="/wiki/Apple_A13" title="Apple A13">A13 processor</a> in a 2019 <a href="/wiki/IPhone_11" title="IPhone 11">iPhone 11</a> performs at 154.9 GFLOPS,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>7<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> a mark supercomputers succeeding the Cray-1 would not reach <a href="/wiki/History_of_supercomputing" title="History of supercomputing">until 1994</a>.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Background">Background</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray-1&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Background"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1320445320">.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+link+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}@media print{body.ns-0 .mw-parser-output .hatnote{display:none!important}}</style><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">Main article: <a href="/wiki/Vector_processor#Supercomputers" title="Vector processor">Vector processor §&#160;Supercomputers</a></div>
<p>Typical scientific workloads consist of reading in large data sets, transforming them in some way and then writing them back out again. Normally the transformations being applied are identical across all of the data points in the set. For instance, the program might add 5 to every number in a set of a million numbers.
</p><p>In simple computers the program would loop over all million numbers, adding five, thereby executing a million instructions saying <code>a = add b, c</code>. Internally the computer solves this instruction in several steps. First it reads the instruction from memory and decodes it, then it collects any additional information it needs, in this case the numbers b and c, and then finally runs the operation and stores the results. The end result is that the computer requires tens or hundreds of millions of cycles to carry out these operations.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Vector_machines">Vector machines</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray-1&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Vector machines"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>In the STAR, new instructions essentially wrote the loops for the user. The user told the machine where in memory the list of numbers was stored, then fed in a single instruction <code>a(1..1000000) = addv b(1..1000000), c(1..1000000)</code>. At first glance it appears the savings are limited; in this case the machine fetches and decodes only a single instruction instead of 1,000,000, thereby saving 1,000,000 fetches and decodes, perhaps one-fourth of the overall time.
</p><p>The real savings are not so obvious. Internally, the <a href="/wiki/Central_processing_unit" title="Central processing unit">CPU</a> of the computer is built up from a number of separate parts dedicated to a single task, for instance, adding a number, or fetching from memory. Normally, as the instruction flows through the machine, only one part is active at any given time. This means that each sequential step of the entire process must complete before a result can be saved. The addition of an <a href="/wiki/Instruction_pipeline" class="mw-redirect" title="Instruction pipeline">instruction pipeline</a> changes this. In such machines the CPU will "look ahead" and begin fetching succeeding instructions while the current instruction is still being processed. In this <a href="/wiki/Assembly_line" title="Assembly line">assembly line</a> fashion any one instruction still requires as long to complete, but as soon as it finishes executing, the next instruction is right behind it, with most of the steps required for its execution already completed.
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Vector_processor" title="Vector processor">Vector processors</a> use this technique with one additional trick. Because the data layout is in a known format&#160;— a set of numbers arranged sequentially in memory&#160;— the pipelines can be tuned to improve the performance of fetches. On the receipt of a vector instruction, special hardware sets up the memory access for the arrays and stuffs the data into the processor as fast as possible.
</p><p>CDC's approach in the STAR used what is today known as a <i>memory-memory architecture</i>. This referred to the way the machine gathered data. It set up its pipeline to read from and write to memory directly. This allowed the STAR to use vectors of length not limited by the length of registers, making it highly flexible. Unfortunately, the pipeline had to be very long in order to allow it to have enough instructions in flight to make up for the slow memory. That meant the machine incurred a high cost when switching from processing vectors to performing operations on non-vector operands. Additionally, the low scalar performance of the machine meant that after the switch had taken place and the machine was running scalar instructions, the performance was quite poor<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (September 2012)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>. The result was rather disappointing real-world performance, something that could, perhaps, have been forecast by <a href="/wiki/Amdahl%27s_law" title="Amdahl&#39;s law">Amdahl's law</a>.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Cray's_approach"><span id="Cray.27s_approach"></span>Cray's approach</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray-1&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Cray&#039;s approach"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Cray studied the failure of the STAR and learned from it<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2020)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>. He decided that in addition to fast vector processing, his design would also require excellent all-around scalar performance. That way when the machine switched modes, it would still provide superior performance. Additionally he noticed that the workloads could be dramatically improved in most cases through the use of <a href="/wiki/Processor_register" title="Processor register">registers</a>.
</p><p>Just as earlier machines had ignored the fact that most operations were being applied to many data points, the STAR ignored the fact that those same data points would be repeatedly operated on. Whereas the STAR would read and process the same memory five times to apply five vector operations on a set of data, it would be much faster to read the data into the CPU's registers once, and then apply the five operations. However, there were limitations with this approach. Registers were significantly more expensive in terms of circuitry, so only a limited number could be provided. This implied that Cray's design would have less flexibility in terms of vector sizes. Instead of reading any sized vector several times as in the STAR, the Cray-1 would have to read only a portion of the vector at a time, but it could then run several operations on that data prior to writing the results back to memory. Given typical workloads, Cray felt that the small cost incurred by being required to break large sequential memory accesses into segments was a cost well worth paying.
</p><p>Since the typical vector operation would involve loading a small set of data into the vector registers and then running several operations on it, the vector system of the new design had its own separate pipeline. For instance, the multiplication and addition units were implemented as separate hardware, so the results of one could be internally pipelined into the next, the instruction decode having already been handled in the machine's main pipeline. Cray referred to this concept as <i>chaining</i>, as it allowed programmers to "chain together" several instructions and extract higher performance.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Performance">Performance</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray-1&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Performance"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>In 1978, a team from the <a href="/wiki/Argonne_National_Laboratory" title="Argonne National Laboratory">Argonne National Laboratory</a> tested a variety of typical workloads on a Cray-1 as part of a proposal to purchase one for their use, replacing their <a href="/wiki/IBM_System/370" title="IBM System/370">IBM 370/195</a>. They also planned on testing on the <a href="/wiki/CDC_STAR-100" title="CDC STAR-100">CDC STAR-100</a> and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Burroughs_Scientific_Computer&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Burroughs Scientific Computer (page does not exist)">Burroughs Scientific Computer</a>, but such tests, if they were performed, were not published. The tests were run on the Cray-1 at the <a href="/wiki/National_Center_for_Atmospheric_Research" title="National Center for Atmospheric Research">National Center for Atmospheric Research</a> (NCAR) in <a href="/wiki/Boulder,_Colorado" title="Boulder, Colorado">Boulder, Colorado</a>. The only other Cray available at the time was the one at Los Alamos, but accessing this machine required <a href="/wiki/Q_clearance" title="Q clearance">Q clearance</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-eval&#95;8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-eval-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>The tests were reported in two ways. The first was a minimum conversion needed to get the program running without errors, but making no attempt to take advantage of the Cray's vectorization. The second included a moderate set of updates to the code, often unwinding loops so they could be vectorized. Generally, the minimal conversions ran roughly the same speed as the 370 to about 2 times its performance (mostly due to a larger exponent range on the Cray), but vectorization led to further increases between 2.5 and 10 times. In one example program, which performed an internal <a href="/wiki/Fast_Fourier_transform" title="Fast Fourier transform">fast Fourier transform</a>, performance improved from the IBM's 47 milliseconds to 3.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-eval&#95;8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-eval-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Description">Description</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray-1&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Description"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-right" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a href="/wiki/File:Cray-1_momory_board_4K_words.jpg" class="mw-file-description"><img src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5e/Cray-1_momory_board_4K_words.jpg/250px-Cray-1_momory_board_4K_words.jpg" decoding="async" width="250" height="204" class="mw-file-element" srcset="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5e/Cray-1_momory_board_4K_words.jpg/500px-Cray-1_momory_board_4K_words.jpg 2x" data-file-width="4933" data-file-height="4016" /></a><figcaption>Memory board, other side is the same - holds 4,096 64-bit words.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The new machine was the first Cray design to use <a href="/wiki/Integrated_circuit" title="Integrated circuit">integrated circuits</a> (ICs). Although ICs had been available since the 1960s, it was only in the early 1970s that they reached the performance necessary for high-speed applications. The Cray-1 used only four different IC types, an <a href="/wiki/Emitter-coupled_logic" title="Emitter-coupled logic">emitter-coupled logic</a> (ECL) dual 5-4 <a href="/wiki/NOR_gate" title="NOR gate">NOR gate</a> (one 5-input, and one 4-input, each with differential output),<sup id="cite&#95;ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>9<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> another slower <a href="/wiki/Emitter-coupled_logic" title="Emitter-coupled logic">MECL</a> 10K 5-4 NOR gate used for address <a href="/wiki/Fanout" class="mw-redirect" title="Fanout">fanout</a>, a 16×4-bit high speed (6&#160;ns) <a href="/wiki/Static_RAM" class="mw-redirect" title="Static RAM">static RAM</a> (SRAM) used for registers and a 1,024×1-bit 48&#160;ns SRAM used for the main memory. These integrated circuits were supplied by <a href="/wiki/Fairchild_Semiconductor" title="Fairchild Semiconductor">Fairchild Semiconductor</a> and <a href="/wiki/Motorola" title="Motorola">Motorola</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-russell&#95;10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-russell-10"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>10<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> In all, the Cray-1 contained about 200,000 gates.
</p><p>ICs were mounted on large five-layer <a href="/wiki/Printed_circuit_board" title="Printed circuit board">printed circuit boards</a>, with up to 144 ICs per board. Boards were then mounted back to back for cooling (see below) and placed in twenty-four 28-inch-high (710&#160;mm) racks containing 72 double-boards. The typical module (distinct processing unit) required one or two boards. In all the machine contained 1,662 modules in 113 varieties.
</p><p>Each cable between the modules was a <a href="/wiki/Twisted_pair" title="Twisted pair">twisted pair</a>, cut to a specific length in order to guarantee the signals arrived at precisely the right time and minimize electrical reflection. Each signal produced by the ECL circuitry was a differential pair, so the signals were balanced. This tended to make the demand on the power supply more constant and reduce switching noise. The load on the power supply was so evenly balanced that Cray boasted that the power supply was unregulated. To the power supply, the entire computer system looked like a simple resistor.
</p><p>The high-performance <a href="/wiki/Emitter-coupled_logic" title="Emitter-coupled logic">ECL</a> circuitry generated considerable heat, and Cray's designers spent as much effort on the design of the refrigeration system as they did on the rest of the mechanical design. In this case, each circuit board was paired with a second, placed back to back with a sheet of copper between them. The copper sheet conducted heat to the edges of the cage, where liquid <a href="/wiki/Freon" title="Freon">Freon</a> running in stainless steel pipes drew it away to the cooling unit below the machine. The first Cray-1 was delayed six months due to problems in the cooling system; lubricant that is normally mixed with the Freon to keep the compressor running would leak through the seals and eventually coat the boards with oil until they shorted out. New welding techniques had to be used to properly seal the tubing.
</p><p>In order to bring maximum speed out of the machine, the entire chassis was bent into a large C-shape. Speed-dependent portions of the system were placed on the "inside edge" of the chassis, where the wire-lengths were shorter. This allowed the cycle time to be decreased to 12.5&#160;ns (80&#160;MHz), not as fast as the 8&#160;ns 8600 he had given up on, but fast enough to beat <a href="/wiki/CDC_7600" title="CDC 7600">CDC 7600</a> and the STAR. NCAR estimated that the overall throughput on the system was 4.5 times that of the CDC 7600.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>11<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>The Cray-1 was built as a <a href="/wiki/64-bit_computing" title="64-bit computing">64-bit</a> system, a departure from the 7600/6600, which were 60-bit machines (a change was also planned for the 8600). Addressing was 24-bit, with a maximum of 1,048,576 64-bit words (1 megaword) of main memory, where each word also had eight parity bits for a total of 72 bits per word.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-brochure&#95;12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-brochure-12"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>12<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>  Memory was spread across 16 <a href="/wiki/Interleaved_memory" title="Interleaved memory">interleaved memory</a> banks, each with a 50&#160;ns cycle time, allowing up to four words to be read per cycle. Smaller configurations could have 0.25 or 0.5 megawords of main memory. Maximum aggregate memory bandwidth was 638&#160;Mbit/s.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-brochure&#95;12-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-brochure-12"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>12<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>The main register set consisted of eight 64-bit scalar (S) registers and eight 24-bit address (A) registers. These were backed by a set of sixty-four registers each for S and A temporary storage known as T and B respectively, which could not be seen by the functional units. The vector system added another eight 64-element by 64-bit vector (V) registers, as well as a vector length (VL) and vector mask (VM). Finally, the system also included a 64-bit real-time clock register and four 64-bit instruction buffers that held sixty-four 16-bit instructions each. The hardware was set up to allow the vector registers to be fed at one word per cycle, while the address and scalar registers required two cycles. In contrast, the entire 16-word instruction buffer could be filled in four cycles.
</p><p>The Cray-1 had twelve pipelined functional units. The 24-bit address arithmetic was performed in an add unit and a multiply unit. The scalar portion of the system consisted of an add unit, a logical unit, a <a href="/wiki/Hamming_weight" title="Hamming weight">population count</a>, a leading zero count unit and a shift unit. The vector portion consisted of add, logical and shift units. The floating point functional units were shared between the scalar and vector portions, and these consisted of add, multiply and reciprocal approximation units.
</p><p>The system had limited parallelism. It could issue one instruction per clock cycle, for a theoretical performance of 80&#160;<a href="/wiki/Instructions_per_second" title="Instructions per second">MIPS</a>, but with vector floating-point multiplication and addition occurring in parallel theoretical performance was 160<sup id="cite&#95;ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>13<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>&#160;MFLOPS. (The reciprocal approximation unit could also operate in parallel, but did not deliver a true floating-point result - two additional multiplications were needed to achieve a full division.)
</p><p>Since the machine was designed to operate on large data sets, the design also dedicated considerable circuitry to <a href="/wiki/Input/output" title="Input/output">I/O</a>. Earlier Cray designs at CDC had included separate computers dedicated to this task, but this was no longer needed. Instead the Cray-1 included four six-channel controllers, each of which was given access to main memory once every four cycles. The channels were 16 bits wide and included three control bits and four bits for error correction, so the maximum transfer speed was one word per 100&#160;ns, or 500 thousand words per second for the entire machine.
</p><p>The initial model, the <b>Cray-1A</b>, weighed 10,500 pounds (4,800&#160;kg) including the Freon refrigeration system. Configured with 1 million <a href="/wiki/Word_(data_type)" class="mw-redirect" title="Word (data type)">words</a> of main memory, the machine and its power supplies consumed about 115&#160;kW of power;<sup id="cite&#95;ref-russell&#95;10-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-russell-10"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>10<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> cooling and storage likely more than doubled this figure.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (November 2010)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> A <a href="/wiki/Data_General" title="Data General">Data General</a> <a href="/wiki/Data_General_Nova" title="Data General Nova">SuperNova S/200</a> minicomputer served as the maintenance control unit (MCU), which was used to feed the <a href="/wiki/Cray_Operating_System" title="Cray Operating System">Cray Operating System</a> into the system at boot time, to monitor the CPU during use, and optionally as a front-end computer. Most, if not all, Cray-1As were delivered using the follow-on <a href="/wiki/Data_General_Eclipse" title="Data General Eclipse">Data General Eclipse</a> as the MCU.
</p><p>The reliability of the CRAY-1A was very low by today's standards. At the <a href="/wiki/European_Centre_for_Medium-Range_Weather_Forecasts" title="European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts">European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts</a>, which was one of the first customers, the mean time between hardware faults was reported to be 96 hours in 1979.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>14<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> Seymour Cray deliberately made design decisions that sacrificed reliability for speed, but improved his later designs after being questioned on this matter. Similarly, the Cray Operating System (COS) was fairly rudimentary, hardly tested and updated weekly or even daily in the early days.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Cray-1S">Cray-1S</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray-1&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Cray-1S"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>The <b>Cray-1S</b>, announced in 1979, was an improved Cray-1 that supported a larger main memory of 1, 2 or 4 million words. The larger main memory was made possible through the use of 4,096 x 1-bit bipolar RAM ICs with a 25&#160;ns access time.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-kolodzey&#95;15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-kolodzey-15"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>15<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> The Data General minicomputers were optionally replaced with an in-house 16-bit design running at 80&#160;MIPS. The I/O subsystem was separated from the main machine, connected to the main system via a 6&#160;Mbit/s control channel and a 100&#160;Mbit/s High Speed Data Channel. This separation made the 1S look like two "half Crays" separated by a few feet, which allowed the I/O system to be expanded as needed. Systems could be bought in a variety of configurations from the S/500 with no I/O and 0.5 million words of memory to the S/4400 with four I/O processors and 4 million words of memory.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Cray-1M">Cray-1M</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray-1&amp;action=edit&amp;section=8" title="Edit section: Cray-1M"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>The <b>Cray-1M</b>, announced in 1982, replaced the Cray-1S.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>16<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> It had a faster 12&#160;ns cycle time and used less expensive <a href="/wiki/MOSFET" title="MOSFET">MOS</a> RAM in the main memory. The 1M was supplied in only three versions, the M/1200 with 1 million words in 8 banks, or the M/2200 and M/4200 with 2 or 4 million words in 16 banks. All of these machines included two, three or four I/O processors, and the system added an optional second High Speed Data Channel. Users could add a <a href="/wiki/Solid-state_drive" title="Solid-state drive">Solid-state Storage Device</a> with 8 to 32 million words of MOS RAM.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Software">Software</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray-1&amp;action=edit&amp;section=9" title="Edit section: Software"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>In 1978, the first standard software package for the Cray-1 was released, consisting of three main products:
</p>
<ul><li><a href="/wiki/Cray_Operating_System" title="Cray Operating System">Cray Operating System</a> (COS) (later machines would run <a href="/wiki/UNICOS" title="UNICOS">UNICOS</a>, Cray's <a href="/wiki/Unix" title="Unix">UNIX</a> flavor)</li>
<li><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray_Assembly_Language&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Cray Assembly Language (page does not exist)">Cray Assembly Language</a> (CAL)</li>
<li><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cray_FORTRAN&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Cray FORTRAN (page does not exist)">Cray FORTRAN</a> (CFT), the first <a href="/wiki/Automatic_vectorization" title="Automatic vectorization">automatically vectorizing</a> <a href="/wiki/Fortran" title="Fortran">Fortran</a> compiler</li></ul>
<p>The <a href="/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy" title="United States Department of Energy">United States Department of Energy</a> funded sites from <a href="/wiki/Lawrence_Livermore_National_Laboratory" title="Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory">Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory</a>, <a href="/wiki/Los_Alamos_Scientific_Laboratory" class="mw-redirect" title="Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory">Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory</a>, <a href="/wiki/Sandia_National_Laboratories" title="Sandia National Laboratories">Sandia National Laboratories</a> and the <a href="/wiki/National_Science_Foundation" title="National Science Foundation">National Science Foundation</a> <a href="/wiki/Supercomputer" title="Supercomputer">supercomputer</a> centers (for high-energy physics) represented the second largest block with LLL's <a href="/wiki/Cray_Time_Sharing_System" title="Cray Time Sharing System">Cray Time Sharing System</a> (CTSS). CTSS was written in a dynamic memory Fortran, first named LRLTRAN, which ran on <a href="/wiki/CDC_7600" title="CDC 7600">CDC 7600s</a>, renamed CVC (pronounced "Civic") when vectorization for the Cray-1 was added. Cray Research attempted to support these sites accordingly. These software choices had influences on later <a href="/wiki/Minisupercomputer" title="Minisupercomputer">minisupercomputers</a>, also known as "<a href="/w/index.php?title=Crayettes&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Crayettes (page does not exist)">crayettes</a>".
</p><p>NCAR has its own operating system (NCAROS).
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/National_Security_Agency" title="National Security Agency">National Security Agency</a> developed its own operating system (Folklore) and language (IMP with ports of Cray Pascal and C and Fortran 90 later)<sup id="cite&#95;ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>17<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>Libraries started with Cray Research's own offerings and <a href="/wiki/Netlib" title="Netlib">Netlib</a>.
</p><p>Other operating systems existed, but most languages tended to be Fortran or Fortran-based. <a href="/wiki/Bell_Labs" title="Bell Labs">Bell Laboratories</a>, as proof of both portability concept and circuit design, moved the first C compiler to their Cray-1 (non-vectorizing). This act would later give CRI a six-month head start on the <a href="/wiki/Cray-2" title="Cray-2">Cray-2</a> Unix port to <a href="/wiki/ETA_Systems" title="ETA Systems">ETA Systems</a>' detriment, and <a href="/wiki/Lucasfilm" title="Lucasfilm">Lucasfilm</a>'s first computer generated test film, <i><a href="/wiki/The_Adventures_of_Andr%C3%A9_%26_Wally_B." title="The Adventures of André &amp; Wally B.">The Adventures of André &amp; Wally B.</a></i>.
</p><p>Application software generally tends to be either classified (<i>e.g.</i> nuclear code, cryptanalytic code) or proprietary (<i>e.g.</i> petroleum reservoir modeling). This was because little software was shared between customers and university customers. The few exceptions were climatological and meteorological programs until the NSF responded to the Japanese <a href="/wiki/Fifth_generation_computer" class="mw-redirect" title="Fifth generation computer">Fifth Generation Computer Systems project</a> and created its supercomputer centers. Even then, little code was shared.
</p><p>Partly because Cray were interested in the publicity, they supported the development of <a href="/wiki/Cray_Blitz" title="Cray Blitz">Cray Blitz</a> which won the fourth (1983) and fifth (1986) <a href="/wiki/World_Computer_Chess_Championship" title="World Computer Chess Championship">World Computer Chess Championship</a>, as well as the 1983 and 1984 <a href="/wiki/North_American_Computer_Chess_Championship" title="North American Computer Chess Championship">North American Computer Chess Championship</a>. The program, <a href="/wiki/Chess_(Northwestern_University)" title="Chess (Northwestern University)"> Chess</a>, that dominated in the 1970s ran on Control Data Corporation supercomputers.
</p>
